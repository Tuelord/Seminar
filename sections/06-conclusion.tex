\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This report examined Uber's Michelangelo as a case study in the software
architecture of MLOps platforms. \Cref{sec:mlops-concepts} showed that it
realises six core MLOps concepts, from an explicit ML lifecycle to
drift monitoring, and \Cref{sec:michelangelo-overview} traced its
evolution from predictive ML to generative AI.

\Cref{sec:design-rationale} showed that Michelangelo's main components
follow directly from six problems Uber faced in 2015, including fragmented
tools, desktop-sized training, the lack of experiment tracking and of a
deployment path, and duplicated feature work~\cite{delbalso2017meet}. The
resulting decisions (a shared feature store, one feature DSL for training
and serving, distributed training, a versioned model repository with
tag-based deployment, and a shared prediction service) target
scalability, low latency, reusability, reproducibility and deployability.

\Cref{sec:pros-cons} showed that these benefits and their costs stem from
the same decisions. Centralisation delivered feature reuse, a
standardised workflow, high scale and project tiering, but also made the
platform heavyweight for prototyping, tightly coupled to Uber's
infrastructure, and exposed to platform-wide failures such as the 2021
Palette metadata incident~\cite{delbalso2018scaling,uber2024palette}.
Uber's response, an easy-to-use option alongside a high-scale one,
illustrates the central trade-off between standardisation and iteration
speed~\cite{delbalso2018scaling}. Compared with TFX and Metaflow, which
are frameworks operated by each adopting team, Michelangelo is a
centralised service; the two approaches are, however, converging.

\Cref{sec:layered-architecture} interpreted Michelangelo~2.0 as a layered
architecture whose core is a control plane over offline and online data
planes~\cite{wang2024predictive}, with other styles, such as
pipe-and-filter, plug-in engines and event-driven reconciliation, inside
individual layers. Layering let Uber migrate infrastructure and replace
engines, for example Spark-based trainers with Ray-based ones, without
disturbing users~\cite{wang2024predictive}, at the cost of fragile shared
layers, per-hop latency and rigidity.

Two general lessons emerge. First, the dominant engineering challenge of
an ML platform lies in data and features rather than in training, which
justifies treating the feature store as a first-class architectural
element~\cite{delbalso2018scaling}. Second, consistency and
reproducibility are best enforced architecturally, through shared
definitions, a single feature code path and immutable model
versions~\cite{delbalso2017meet,sun2020gallery}, while selectively opened
layers preserve flexibility for advanced users. Because Michelangelo is
proprietary, this analysis relies on Uber's publications rather than
direct inspection; future work could examine its LLMOps capabilities in
more depth.
