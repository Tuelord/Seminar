\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This report examined Uber's Michelangelo as a case study in the software
architecture of MLOps platforms. Section~\ref{sec:mlops-concepts} showed
that the platform provides a concrete realisation of six core MLOps
concepts: an explicit ML lifecycle, CI/CD and continuous training, a
feature store, a model registry, safeguards against training/serving
skew, and continuous monitoring for drift. Its evolution from predictive
ML on tabular data, through deep learning, to generative AI demonstrates
that a centralised platform can absorb successive shifts in ML technology
without being rebuilt from scratch~\cite{wang2024predictive}.

The design rationale in Section~\ref{sec:design-rationale} traces the
platform's main components back to concrete problems Uber faced in 2015:
fragmented tools, untracked experiments, the absence of a standard
deployment path and training limited to a single
machine~\cite{delbalso2017meet}. Shared features motivated the feature
store, one-click deployment motivated the model repository with tagged
models, consistency between training and serving motivated a single
feature DSL, and scale motivated distributed engines such as Spark,
Horovod and, later, Ray. These decisions target scalability, low serving
latency, reuse and reproducibility as the primary quality attributes.

The analysis in Section~\ref{sec:pros-cons} shows that these benefits come
at a cost. Centralisation enables reuse, standardised workflows and
tiered support for projects of different business impact, but it also
makes the platform heavy, couples it to Uber-specific infrastructure, and
initially slowed experimentation through Scala-, configuration- and
UI-based workflows~\cite{delbalso2017meet,delbalso2018scaling,wang2024predictive}. Uber's
own lessons reinforce this picture: data is the hardest part of ML, mature
open-source components such as Spark and Cassandra each required more
than a year of effort to run reliably at Uber's scale, and the platform
converged on a hybrid offering that combines an easy-to-use option with a
high-scale option~\cite{delbalso2018scaling}.

Section~\ref{sec:layered-architecture} interpreted Michelangelo~2.0 as a
layered architecture with a control plane, an offline data plane and an
online data plane~\cite{wang2024predictive}. Separating declarative
entity management from batch and real-time execution allows execution
engines to be replaced behind stable interfaces, as illustrated by the
replacement of Spark-based trainers with Ray-based
ones~\cite{wang2024predictive}; other styles, such as pipelines, the
lambda pattern for batch and streaming features, and plug-in execution
engines, are contained within individual layers.

Three general lessons emerge for architects of ML platforms. First, the
dominant engineering challenge lies in data and feature management
rather than in model training, which justifies treating the feature
store as a first-class architectural element. Second, consistency between
offline and online paths is best enforced architecturally, through shared
definitions and a single code path, rather than by convention; likewise,
reproducibility proved central to model management even though Gallery
was not originally designed for it~\cite{sun2020gallery}. Third, a
platform must balance standardisation against flexibility; Michelangelo
addresses this through modular, plug-and-play components and a two-level
offering of predefined workflow templates for most users and low-level
infrastructure access for advanced users~\cite{wang2024predictive}.

This study has two main limitations. Michelangelo is proprietary, so our
analysis relies on Uber's publications and engineering reports rather
than on direct inspection, and our demonstrator approximates its
components with open-source tools. Future work could extend the analysis
to the platform's LLMOps capabilities and compare it empirically with
open-source alternatives.
